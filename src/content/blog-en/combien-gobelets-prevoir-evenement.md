The number of tickets sold doesn't, on its own, tell you how many cups to plan for. A visitor may have several drinks, keep their cup or hand it back quickly. Meanwhile, part of the stock is waiting to be returned, washed or redistributed.

To size a large event, keep two figures apart: the total volume of drinks served and the clean stock you need between two restocks.

## Start from the pace of sales

Use your bar history where you have it: sales per time slot, sizes used, footfall in the different areas and differences between days. For a first edition, build several scenarios rather than a single ratio presented as a certainty.

Peaks matter more than a daily average. The same number of drinks spread over ten hours doesn't create the same pressure as the same number served in one rush after a concert. Bars that are far away and delivery access points also need to be taken into account.

The forecast total helps you estimate the washing. The peak in demand, combined with the time it takes for clean cups to come back, helps you estimate the cup stock.

## Calculate the stock you need between two returns of clean cups

A working basis is:

**Stock needed for a period = service needs before the next restock − clean returns actually available + operational reserve.**

Fictional example: you expect 1,500 drinks to be served before the next delivery. You have 500 clean cups whose return is confirmed before the peak. With a chosen reserve of 300 pieces, the extra stock to plan for is 1,300 cups.

This example doesn't set a universal reserve rate. Your margin depends on the level of risk you can accept, transport time, how reliable your forecasts are and the stock already spread across the bars.

Dirty returns should never be counted as immediately available. When they go back into circulation depends on the processing schedule you have agreed.

## Keep sizes and uses separate

Work out the needs for 25 cl cups, 50 cl cups and any other containers separately. A large stock of one size doesn't automatically make up for running out of another.

Also check the site rules, the drinks on offer and whether each model is suitable for its intended use. A cup designed for cold drinks shouldn't be used for hot drinks without checking the manufacturer's conditions.

At a sports event, serving points can be far apart and open at different times. At a Christmas market, several weekends in a row can justify a buffer stock that is independent of the weekday rotations.

## Test the scenario where things go less well

Ask three questions before you confirm the stock: what happens if more people come than expected, if fewer cups come back, or if a delivery arrives later than planned?

Decide which part of the reserve can be moved, who authorises its release and at what threshold a manager is alerted. A reserve is only useful if the teams know where it is and can get to it.

A clearly explained deposit system and visible return points can help you get cups back. Track the quantities returned without lumping together losses, cups kept by visitors and counting gaps: each of these calls for a different response.

Send your needs per day, your existing stock, the peak times and the rotations you would like. The provider can then check the quantities available and the proposed schedule; this calculation does not count as a booking.
