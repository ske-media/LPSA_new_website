A reusable cup doesn't, on its own, tell the story of an event's environmental footprint. You need to know how many times it circulates, how many pieces are recovered and how washing and transport are organised.

For a credible CSR approach, start with data your team can verify. It will help you improve the set-up and communicate without turning an estimate into a measured result.

## Count what goes out and what comes back

At each event, record the number of cups handed to the serving points, the quantities recovered and the stock left unused. Note the method used: counting each cup, standardised crates or an estimate.

The return rate needs a stable definition. You can, for example, compare the cups recovered with those actually handed out, provided you can measure both numbers consistently.

Keep cups kept by visitors, identified losses, damaged cups and counting gaps separate. An overall gap doesn't yet explain what happened.

## Track how the cup stock is used over time

Adding up how many times the stock goes out across several events lets you follow how it circulates. Be clear, however, about whether you are counting issues, washes or drinks served: these are different units.

Fictional example: a stock of 600 pieces with 3,000 identified uses represents an average of five uses per piece across the stock. This calculation doesn't prove that each cup was used exactly five times; some may have circulated more than others.

Our [reuse scenario calculator](/en/rse/) helps you explore this type of assumption. Its results should be presented as scenarios when they aren't based on your real data.

## Document washing and logistics

Record the quantities sent for washing, the dates, the journeys and how the cups were packed. If you want to analyse water or energy use, ask what data the provider can supply and what scope it covers.

A workshop's total consumption is not automatically a consumption per cup. The allocation method and the operations included need to be defined before you publish a figure.

Distance matters too, but it doesn't describe everything about transport. The vehicle, the load and grouped rounds change the context. These elements are there to prepare an analysis, not to announce a universal environmental benefit.

## Avoid shortcuts in equivalences

A large number of uses doesn't automatically mean the same number of disposable cups avoided. This equivalence requires a reference scenario: which container would have been used without the cup stock, and would a disposable container have been used once or several times?

In the same way, an observed drop in waste isn't enough to put a figure on CO₂ savings. A life cycle assessment looks in particular at manufacturing, use, washing, transport and end of life.

The [RDC Environment study on cups at events, published in 2013](https://www.lecollectifdesfestivals.org/collectif/download/Impact_gobelets_RDC_2013.pdf), illustrates this scenario-based approach. It is not a current measure of La Plonge s’Adapte's performance and cannot be used to set a universal reuse threshold for every event.

## Link social impact to verifiable facts

Working with a French ‘entreprise adaptée’ (a company employing mainly people with disabilities) also gives your purchase a social dimension. Describe the service you entrusted to the company and its role, without arbitrarily converting an invoice into jobs created or hours of employment support.

For the employers concerned, the [OETH scheme (France's obligation to employ disabled workers) and purchases from an entreprise adaptée](/en/blog/entreprise-adaptee-oeth-lavage-gobelets/) fall under a specific framework, separate from the environmental assessment.

## Put a few indicators in your report

Present the cup stock, the uses tracked, the returns, the pieces set aside and the rotations completed. Add the period, the counting method and the limits of the data. You'll have a useful basis for improving the next edition.
