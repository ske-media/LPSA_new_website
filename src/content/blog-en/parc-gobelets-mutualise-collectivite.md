A group of municipalities or an urban community can buy a cup stock for the events held in its area. The benefit is that shared equipment circulates between several organisers. What really makes the difference is the circuit that allows this equipment to be collected, washed and made available again.

A shared cup stock becomes a service once responsibilities and the schedule are clearly defined.

## List the needs before choosing the volume

Start with the events concerned: village fêtes, sports events, markets, festivals and community gatherings. Note their dates, the drink sizes they use and their estimated volumes.

Events taking place at the same time need particular attention. A stock used on Saturday won't automatically be available to another association on Sunday. The sizing must take into account travel, return times and washing.

Also look at the uses that come back regularly. A small number of common sizes and a design that remains valid for several years can make tracking easier. Our [buy or rent comparison](/en/blog/acheter-ou-louer-gobelets-reutilisables/) helps you set out the business model.

## Decide who owns and who organises

The local authority can own the cup stock and be responsible for allocating it. An operator can handle the washing and storage under the agreed contract. These roles should be written down separately.

Specify who takes bookings, who confirms availability, who hands the batches over to associations and who receives the returns. La Plonge s’Adapte can look at washing and storing a cup stock owned by a local authority; managing loans or bookings should not be assumed to be included.

Appoint one contact person for the local authority and one for each event. Changes of volunteers shouldn't wipe out the record of the quantities handed over.

## Write a common set of rules for lending

The sheet given to the organiser can specify:

- the sizes and quantities provided;
- the dates and places for handover and return;
- the containers and instructions for preparing the batches;
- the counting rule and how losses or damage are handled;
- how washing and transport costs are shared;
- who to contact in case of delay or a change in volume.

Also decide what happens when visitors keep their cups. The deposit, the return arrangements and any charges should remain easy for every association to understand.

## Treat clean stock as a shared resource

Plan the calendar with an overview of confirmed events, batches in use, expected returns and clean batches available. Knowing where a stock is doesn't always tell you what state it is in: a crate that has just come back from a fête is not a batch ready to lend.

Storage must be defined together with storage conditions, batch labelling and inventories. Access and withdrawals should be arranged with the operator concerned; they shouldn't depend on a single person who is the only one to know where the crates are.

## Review the service after the first season

Track the quantities provided, collected, washed and set aside, as well as the number of events served. These indicators allow you to adjust the cup stock and the rules of use.

They describe how the service works; they are not enough to automatically calculate CO₂ savings. For solid environmental communication, see our [guide to reuse indicators](/en/blog/rse-gobelets-reemploi-indicateurs/).

The [rental services with washing offered by local authorities, such as SDED 52](https://www.sded52.fr/gobelets-reutilisables), show how important it is to build these operations into the scheme. Each area must, however, define its own scope and conditions.
