A Christmas market keeps its cups in use for several weeks. Weekend peaks, changes of staff and the needs of each chalet make regular returns just as important as the starting quantity.

Good preparation means setting up a loop that holds up over time: clean stock, distribution, collection, washing, then making the cups available again according to a confirmed schedule.

## Choose cups suitable for hot drinks

Before you buy or rent, specify which drinks will be served and at what temperatures. Choosing a reusable cup doesn't guarantee that it is suitable for mulled wine, tea or hot chocolate.

Ask for the manufacturer's instructions and the relevant compliance documents. They should let you check the intended uses, in particular the foods, the temperatures and the fact that the cup will be used repeatedly. The [DGCCRF, the French consumer protection authority, sets out these points for materials in contact with food](https://www.economie.gouv.fr/dgccrf/les-fiches-pratiques/les-regles-respecter-pour-les-materiaux-emballages-ustensiles-et-contenants-au-contact-des-aliments).

The model must also be compatible with the planned washing process. A trial on a sample, using the drinks from your market, is a good step before ordering a large stock. It gives you a chance to discuss stains, how well the print holds up and drying, without promising that no wear will ever occur.

## Build a week-by-week forecast

An average across the whole event hides your real needs. Prepare a table with the expected volumes per day, the weekends, any special events and any days the market is closed.

For each rotation, note when the dirty cups are available, when transport can access the site and when the next clean batch needs to arrive. Add an operational margin agreed with your teams.

Don't assume the whole stock can be used at the same time. Some of it may be in the chalets, some on its way back, some being washed. Our [sizing guide](/en/blog/combien-gobelets-prevoir-evenement/) helps you separate the physical stock from the total number of drinks served.

## Give every chalet the same instructions

Centralise the collection rules: designated containers, sizes kept separate as instructed, drinks emptied, waste removed and the chalet clearly identified. A simple sheet displayed near the bar makes handovers easier when a team changes.

Appoint someone in charge of the crates and set a time for bringing them together. Without this checkpoint, collection can turn into an improvised round of the chalets that are still open.

Batches from different cup stocks should stay clearly identified. This makes inventories easier and limits debates about missing cups at the end of the market.

## Plan for access constraints while the market is open

Identify pedestrian zones, restricted access, delivery slots and where a vehicle can stop. Confirm what handling equipment is available and who can be reached on site.

Transport can be organised as part of the service, but its times must fit the constraints of both the site and the washing provider. A daily or urgent return has to be confirmed before the start; it can't be assumed just from the distance between the two places.

## Plan the closing from day one

The last rotation often includes an inventory, collecting any stock that is still clean and returning accessories. Agree on the counting rule and the rental or storage conditions before the market opens.
