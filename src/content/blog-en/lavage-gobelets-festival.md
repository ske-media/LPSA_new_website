A festival doesn't end with the last encore. Behind the bars, there are still crates to collect, cups to count and a team that has already given a lot. Planning the cup washing before the gates open makes this final stage predictable.

For an event that puts a very large number of cups into circulation, the question goes well beyond washing up: you need to organise a flow between service, returns, washing and the next use.

## Start with the date you need clean cups back

The first question is a simple one: when will you need clean cups again? A three-day festival with a daily rotation calls for a different set-up from an event where the whole stock can leave once it's over.

Give your provider the service dates, the bar closing times, the site access slots and what you need for the following day. A total quantity isn't enough to book a schedule: 3,000 cups handed over in one batch and three batches of 1,000 expected each day don't imply the same pace.

The washing turnaround, transport times and the availability of clean stock need to be confirmed together. A return during the night doesn't automatically mean a delivery the next morning.

## Keep stocks separate, even during the rush

Set up clearly marked areas for clean cups, dirty returns and containers that need checking. The returns circuit should still make sense to someone starting their shift in the middle of the evening.

Give each bar a short instruction: empty out any drinks, remove waste and put the cups in the designated container. The type of crate, how the different sizes are packed and any fill limits should follow the instructions agreed with the washing provider. Try not to let a crate of cups turn into an end-of-shift bin.

Mixed models, forgotten objects and unlabelled crates all add sorting work. They also make counting and returning the stock harder, especially when several associations or bars use different cup stocks.

## Make counting a checkpoint

Appoint one person in charge of cups going out and coming back. A delivery note can record the date, the bar the cups came from, the size, the number of crates and the estimated quantity. Make it clear what is an exact count and what is still an estimate.

The quantity put into circulation, the quantity recovered on site and the quantity finally sent for washing are three different pieces of information. Comparing them helps you understand any gaps without automatically blaming the transport or the provider.

To size your stock between two rotations, see our [method for working out how many cups you need](/en/blog/combien-gobelets-prevoir-evenement/).

## Prepare for the last hour before it arrives

The end of the night is when tasks pile up. Book the collection area, the people handling the crates and the meeting point with the carrier in advance. Also check who still has access to the site once the public has left.

Washing includes cleaning, drying and getting the cups ready for use again. Drying plastic deserves particular care; a cup that looks clean should not be put away while it's still damp. The manufacturer [Winterhalter explains the specific requirements for washing reusable tableware](https://www.winterhalter.com/fr-fr/utilisations/lavage-de-la-vaisselle-reutilisable/).

## What to include in your quote request

Prepare the volume per size, the number of days, any rotations, the collection address, the date you expect the cups back and where the stock comes from. Add any access constraints and whether you can drop the crates off yourself.
